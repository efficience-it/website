A framework or language version reaching end of support is a silent event. No deployment breaks, no alert rings, the application keeps serving traffic. Yet from that date on, the vendor no longer publishes patches, even for critical vulnerabilities. The risk, until then covered by the open source community, shifts to the operator.

This article reviews the updated Symfony and PHP end-of-life calendar, the real consequences and the three exit strategies depending on how critical the application is.

## What an End of Support Really Changes

An end of support, or End of Life (EOL), marks the official end of patch releases by the vendor. For Symfony, the lifecycle calendar is published on the [official Symfony releases page](https://symfony.com/releases). For PHP, the official calendar is published on the [PHP supported versions page](https://www.php.net/supported-versions). These two pages are the sources of truth.

Three operational effects appear from the EOL date. First effect: CVEs discovered after the EOL are no longer fixed on the affected version. The vulnerability stays public and exploitable, and attackers have a scanning tool to identify targets. Second effect: security analysis tools flag the version as non-compliant, which shows up in vendor questionnaires and compliance reviews. Third effect: hosts and Linux distributions gradually remove the version from base images, which complicates redeployments and eventually blocks the CI/CD pipeline.

None of these three effects is immediate on the EOL date. It is a slow degradation that gives a false sense of security for six to eighteen months. Then an incident triggers awareness: a high-profile CVE, an insurance audit, a client questionnaire. At that point, the remediation time is measured in months, and the exposure in the meantime is total.

Symfony 6.4 LTS and 7.4 LTS are the two reasonable targets for the next twelve months. On PHP, 8.4 is now the actively supported version, and 8.3 remains acceptable until the end of 2027 in its security phase.

## Real Consequences of a Version at End of Support

Security is the first consequence, but not the only one. Four risks pile up and reinforce each other as the version debt grows.

### Unpatched Vulnerabilities and CVE Exposure

EOL versions no longer receive patches. Vulnerabilities published since the EOL stay open, and the topic of [PHP dependency CVEs](/article/cve-comprendre-les-failles-pour-mieux-se-proteger) (in French) remains central in any security review. On a medium-sized application with about fifty Composer dependencies, a typical scan of a target unmaintained for eighteen months returns between ten and fifty active CVEs, one to five of them classified as critical: remote code execution, authentication bypass, SQL injection, denial of service.

### Cyber Insurance Refusal and Contractual Exclusions

Cyber insurance contracts have tightened since 2023. Standard clauses require applying vendor patches within a reasonable time and using supported versions. An EOL version in production is a direct breach of these clauses. After an incident, the post-incident expert review identifies the version being run, cross-references it with the exploited CVEs and concludes on coverage. Total exclusion of payout has become the most frequent outcome in claims involving an obsolete stack.

### NIS2, DORA and GDPR Non-Compliance

NIS2 requires essential and important entities to manage vulnerabilities in a documented way, aligned with vendor recommendations. DORA, applicable since January 2025 to financial players, requires a map of critical IT dependencies and an active remediation plan. On the GDPR side, the recommendations of ANSSI and the CNIL converge: keeping an unsupported version of a component that processes personal data is a security failure under Article 32. Audits first look for unsupported versions in production inventories.

### Recruiting Difficulties and Talent Drain

An often underestimated effect: senior Symfony developers avoid assignments on EOL versions. A job post on Symfony 4.4 or PHP 7.4 gets on average two to three times fewer applications than an equivalent post on Symfony 6.4 or PHP 8.3. The profiles that respond are less demanding about quality, which mechanically worsens [technical debt](/article/la-dette-technique-faut-il-vraiment-en-avoir-peur) (in French) over time. ## How to Assess Your Exposure in Thirty Minutes

Before building a plan, you need to quantify the exposure. Three questions are enough for a first diagnosis led by the IT department itself.

First question: which Symfony and PHP versions run in production? The `php -v` command on the application server gives PHP. The Symfony version is readable in `composer.json` or via `bin/console --version`. Good discipline in [using Composer](/article/utilisation-de-composer-dans-le-developpement-symfony-conseils-pratiques) (in French) means this information is aligned across all environments.

Second question: what is the EOL date of each of these versions? The table above gives it for current versions. For older versions, [endoflife.date](https://endoflife.date) remains the aggregation reference.

Third question: what is the gap in months between today and the EOL date? If the gap is negative (EOL passed), the exposure is active and the priority is high. If the gap is under six months, you need an immediate plan. Beyond twelve months, the situation is under control and the topic moves into normal planning.

This diagnosis fits on one A4 page and lets a non-technical executive understand the exposure of their IT system. To go further, our [free 30-minute Symfony audit](/audit-symfony-gratuit) (in French) adds a quick review of the Composer ecosystem and identifies potential migration blockers.

## Three Exit Strategies

Once the exposure is measured, three strategies are possible. The choice depends on how critical the application is, the depth of the technical debt and the maturity of the team.

### Strategy 1: Gradual Minor Updates

The first strategy is to move from minor version to minor version, without changing the major version. It applies to applications close to their LTS (Symfony 6.2 to 6.4 LTS, PHP 8.1 to 8.2). The effort is limited, regressions are contained, and the existing test coverage stays relevant. This strategy does not solve the major jump when it becomes necessary, but it buys time.

### Strategy 2: Planned Major Jump

The second strategy is to plan the jump to the next LTS over three to six months, with a dedicated team and a formal migration plan. This is the standard path to move from Symfony 4.4 to 6.4 LTS, or from PHP 7.4 to 8.3. Our [migration guide for a Symfony project](/article/guide-de-migration-dans-un-projet-symfony) (in French) details the phases: initial audit, Composer dependency upgrade, framework upgrade through intermediate versions, PHP upgrade, regression tests, gradual deployment. [Rector](/article/rector-et-ses-pouvoirs-maitrisez-levolution-de-votre-code-symfony) (in French) tooling automates a significant part of the code transformations, and static analysis with [PHPStan](/article/comment-phpstan-peut-vous-aider-a-ameliorer-la-qualite-de-votre-code-php) (in French) secures each step.

### Strategy 3: Targeted Rebuild

The third strategy only concerns applications whose debt is so heavy that a direct migration costs more effort than a targeted rebuild of the critical modules. It does not mean rewriting everything (the big rewrite is still a bad idea), but isolating the most exposed modules and rebuilding them alongside the existing system. Our approach to [legacy PHP application modernization](/modernisation-application-php) (in French), detailed in the article on [modernizing a legacy PHP application without rewriting everything](/article/moderniser-application-php-legacy-sans-tout-reecrire) (in French), covers this path. It is justified when the application combines an old EOL version and a total absence of tests.

## Typical Timeline of a Major Migration

A major-version Symfony migration (for example 4.4 to 6.4) follows a proven sequence on a medium-sized application, on the order of 50 to 100 person-days of business code.

Phase 1, weeks 1 to 4: initial audit. Mapping of Composer dependencies, identification of abandoned dependencies, measurement of test coverage, static analysis at the highest supported level. Our [PHP quality analysis](/audit-code-php) (in French) frames this phase.

Phase 2, weeks 5 to 8: preparation. Strengthening test coverage on critical modules, replacing abandoned dependencies, cleaning up dead code. Often the longest phase, because it reveals unquantified debt.

Phase 3, weeks 9 to 14: version upgrade. Moving from Symfony 4.4 to 5.4, then 5.4 to 6.4. Each intermediate jump is deployed to staging, validated, then pushed to production. PHP follows the same sequence, in parallel.

Phase 4, weeks 15 to 18: stabilization. Closer monitoring, fixing residual regressions, updating documentation and the CI/CD pipeline. A phase too often compressed even though it determines long-term stability.

Phase 5, ongoing: prevention. Static analysis at the highest level, a semiannual version upgrade calendar, Composer audit alerts. Without this phase, the debt builds up again in under eighteen months.

## Real-World Cases

Two situations come up often. First case: a B2B SaaS on Symfony 4.4 and PHP 7.4, EOL passed for eighteen months, team reduced to one senior developer. The migration to Symfony 6.4 and PHP 8.3 takes five months, with a major effort on test coverage in the first two months. Trigger: a security questionnaire from a large client requiring the list of versions in use.

Second case: an internal application of an industrial group on Symfony 5.4 and PHP 8.1, with a generalist IT team. The Symfony EOL passed six months ago, the PHP EOL four. Management launches an audit after a remark from the compliance auditor. The migration to Symfony 6.4 and PHP 8.3 takes three months with external support. ## Conclusion

The end of support of a Symfony or PHP version is not an event to handle reactively. It is predictable data, published years in advance by the vendor, and it can be planned like any other IT investment cycle. Companies that follow this rhythm move from one LTS to the next without drama, and keep an IT system where security risk stays limited, cyber insurance stays obtainable and compliance stays achievable.

Those that miss this rhythm enter a vicious circle: debt accumulates, talent leaves, and the migration becomes a project in itself. The right reflex is to anticipate: maintain a dashboard with the Symfony version, the PHP version, the EOL date of each, and the gap in months. If that gap turns negative, the organization must trigger a plan without waiting for an incident.
